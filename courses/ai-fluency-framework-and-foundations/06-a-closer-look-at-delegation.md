## The point

Delegation is primarily about the *effective* and *efficient* half of AI Fluency, not the ethical/safe half (that's [A closer look at Diligence](./12-a-closer-look-at-diligence.md)). Core claim worth sitting with: "the cornerstone of good delegation isn't actually about AI at all — it's about your own expertise." The most effective AI collaborators are "experts in their fields first, and AI delegators second."

## The three components of Delegation

- **Problem Awareness** — clearly defining your goals and understanding the work needed *before* bringing AI in at all: what are you trying to accomplish, what does success look like, what kind of thinking/work does it require (simple-but-time-consuming, uncertain-and-needs-a-thinking-partner, data-poor, or requiring critical judgment)?
- **Platform Awareness** — working knowledge of the AI landscape: different systems have different strengths (speed vs. depth, accuracy vs. creativity), and the field moves fast enough that hands-on experimentation matters more than memorizing a snapshot.
- **Task Delegation** — the actual strategic split: what should be automated, what benefits from augmentation, what should stay exclusively human judgment, what can an agent handle independently.

## Ties to the 4Ds

This is the full treatment of Delegation, the first D — introduced briefly in [The 4D Framework](./03-the-4d-framework.md), now given its own three-part internal structure. Explicitly reuses the [Why do we need AI Fluency](./02-why-do-we-need-ai-fluency.md) engagement modes (Automation, Augmentation, Agency) as the menu Task Delegation chooses from.

## Key takeaway to land

Delegation isn't "what can I offload" — it's "what do I actually need," decided *before* AI enters the picture. Skipping Problem Awareness and going straight to Platform/Task Delegation is delegating from a position of not knowing what you're delegating.

## Exercise: Analyze tasks with an AI assistant

**Why:** practices Problem Awareness on something small and low-stakes before the next lesson applies it to a real multi-step project.

1. Pick a simple task (drafting an email, outlining a presentation, planning a meeting).
2. Open a real conversation with Claude — share the task, ask for help building a delegation plan.
3. Work through together: what's the vision/what does success look like, what pieces of work are needed, which pieces need human expertise/creativity/judgment. Have an *actual back-and-forth conversation*, not a one-shot exchange of statements.
4. Produce a simple delegation plan using both your strengths and the AI's.

### A worked example: outlining a presentation for a general audience

**Problem Awareness:** a general-public audience, delivered live. Success = a well-prepared presentation that genuinely informs the audience about effective, efficient, ethical, and safe AI use in daily tasks.

**Task breakdown + delegation, one reasonable split:**
1. Core narrative/structure — collaborative (your vision leads, worked through together).
2. Content per section (the substance of each point) — AI drafts, human reviews/simplifies for a general audience.
3. Audience-relevant examples — collaborative (requires real knowledge of the specific audience the AI has no visibility into, but brainstormed together rather than solo).
4. Format-specific pacing (timing, visual aids, audience-interaction points) — collaborative.
5. Delivery/rehearsal notes — solo, human only.

A pattern worth watching for once you run this yourself: a lot of people practice Delegation by instinct already, typified by a pure Automation-mode pattern ("here's the input, go apply this transformation to it"). Understanding the framework's *why* tends to broaden the sense of when collaboration (Augmentation) would actually serve better than defaulting straight to automation or full agency — especially in technical/coding work, where handing off the whole task is often the reflex even when a genuine back-and-forth ("what do you think about this approach") would serve better.
